# AI fiction tells — the catalogue

**Adopted 2026-09-06** as the fiction half of [plain-writing.md](plain-writing.md).
That doc is the house rule and wins any disagreement; this is the long list of
specific habits to check prose against. The carve-outs in plain-writing.md's
*In-world prose* section apply here too, and [story.md](../story.md) still wins
on voice.

The one line worth keeping in your head:

> Explain the writing less. Trust the writing more.

---

A practical reference for spotting and removing the habits that make AI-generated fiction feel awkward, generic, over-explained, or strangely polished.

None of these are proof that something was written by AI. Humans use all of them. The problem is **frequency, clustering, and predictability**. A single em dash is normal. A paragraph full of em dashes, adjective triplets, thematic explanations, and "not merely" constructions starts to sound generated.

The central problem is this:

> **AI tends to explain the writing instead of trusting the writing.**

Human fiction is often messier, more specific, less symmetrical, and more willing to leave things unsaid.

---

## 1. The "Not X, But Y" Construction

One of the most common AI rhetorical patterns.

> It isn't just a game, but a living world.

> This isn't merely a weapon, but a symbol of oppression.

> The system doesn't simply work; it fundamentally changes how you play.

In fiction, this often becomes especially annoying because the prose keeps announcing the *deeper meaning* of ordinary things.

### Watch for

- not just X, but Y
- not merely X, but Y
- not only X, but also Y
- more than just X
- far more than X
- beyond simply X

### More natural

> The weapon was still warm.

Rather than:

> The weapon wasn't merely a tool of violence, but a reminder of the brutality that had consumed the city.

If the weapon is supposed to symbolize something, let the reader notice it.

---

## 2. Explaining What the Reader Just Saw

This is probably the biggest fictional-writing problem.

### AI

> Angus slammed the door. His anger was obvious from the force with which he closed it.

### Human

> Angus slammed the door.

The second version trusts the reader.

Other examples:

> The character's clenched fist demonstrates his anger.

> The silence creates a sense of tension.

> Her trembling voice conveyed her fear.

Often the action already does the job.

---

## 3. Explaining Jokes

AI frequently refuses to let a joke stand on its own.

### AI

> Angus made a joke about the situation, providing a moment of levity amid the otherwise grim circumstances.

### Better

Write the joke.

Let the reader decide that it was funny.

---

## 4. Explaining Emotions

AI frequently names an emotion immediately after showing it.

> His face tightened with anger, demonstrating his frustration.

> She stared at the body, overcome by a deep sense of sadness.

> His hands trembled, betraying his fear.

Sometimes this is appropriate. When every emotion is explicitly labeled, though, the writing becomes predictable.

### Try instead

Show behavior:

> He read the message twice. Then he folded the paper into a tiny square and put it in his mouth.

The reader can figure out that something is wrong.

---

## 5. Adjective Piles

AI loves stacking adjectives:

> a dark, oppressive, and bleak world

> a strange, unsettling, and mysterious figure

> a brutal, unforgiving, and dangerous city

Often these adjectives overlap.

### Better

Choose one strong word, or replace adjectives with a concrete detail.

Instead of:

> a dark, oppressive, and bleak city

Try:

> The streetlights hadn't worked in six years.

Specificity creates atmosphere better than adjective accumulation.

---

## 6. The Three-Item List Reflex

AI loves threes.

> exploration, combat, and survival

> jobs, housing, and crime

> dark, gritty, and atmospheric

> strange, dangerous, and unpredictable

Triplets are perfectly legitimate in prose. The problem is when everything becomes a carefully balanced three-part list.

Human writing is often lopsided.

> He stole a car, shot a man, and forgot to buy milk.

The third item breaking the expected pattern can feel much more natural than:

> He stole a car, shot a man, and committed several other serious crimes.

---

## 7. Artificially Perfect Symmetry

AI loves balanced constructions:

> simple yet powerful

> brutal yet rewarding

> dark yet humorous

> complex yet accessible

> familiar yet innovative

> both challenging and rewarding

This can make every sentence sound like advertising copy.

Human prose is usually less symmetrical.

> It was simple. It was also a pain in the ass.

---

## 8. "At Its Core"

Extremely common AI phrasing.

> At its core, this is a story about...

> At its core, the character is...

Usually just remove it.

> This is a story about...

---

## 9. "In Many Ways"

Another common filler phrase.

> In many ways, the city itself is a character.

Usually:

> The city is a character.

---

## 10. "Ultimately"

AI loves using this to manufacture a profound conclusion.

> Ultimately, it's about survival.

> Ultimately, the choice is yours.

> Ultimately, what matters is...

If the sentence works without it, remove it.

---

## 11. "At the End of the Day"

Usually sounds like someone giving a corporate presentation rather than writing fiction.

---

## 12. "It's Worth Noting"

Avoid in narrative prose.

> It's worth noting that the eastern district is abandoned.

Just show the eastern district being abandoned.

---

## 13. "Interestingly"

The narrator shouldn't constantly announce what is interesting.

> Interestingly, the corpse was still warm.

Better:

> The corpse was still warm.

The reader gets to decide whether that is interesting.

---

## 14. "Notably"

Same problem.

> Notably, the door was unlocked.

Better:

> The door was unlocked.

---

## 15. "Importantly"

Same.

> Importantly, nobody heard the gunshot.

Better:

> Nobody heard the gunshot.

---

## 16. "Crucially"

Same unless you are writing an actual analysis rather than fiction.

---

## 17. "This Is Where..."

AI loves dramatic transition sentences:

> This is where things get interesting.

> This is where the real story begins.

> This is where the system comes into play.

These are usually stronger when the actual event simply happens.

---

## 18. "That's Where X Comes In"

Another explanatory crutch.

> That's where the Long Watch comes in.

Instead:

> The Long Watch arrived at dusk.

---

## 19. "This Means That..."

AI often explains the implications of the preceding sentence.

> The city had no functioning police force. This meant that residents were forced to protect themselves.

A more natural version:

> The city had no functioning police force. Residents protected themselves.

Or just show the consequences.

---

## 20. "Which Meant..."

Can be useful, but AI often chains these together.

> The generator failed, which meant the lights went out, which meant the doors locked, which meant...

The chain can become mechanical.

Prefer concrete action:

> The generator died. The lights went out. Every door in the building locked.

---

# ATMOSPHERE AND DESCRIPTION

## 21. Generic Horror Adjectives

AI loves:

- ominous
- foreboding
- sinister
- unsettling
- eerie
- chilling
- haunting
- grotesque
- macabre
- nightmarish
- disturbing

None are bad words. The problem is using them instead of describing something.

### AI

> The abandoned hospital had an eerie, unsettling atmosphere.

### Better

> Someone had left the lights on in the operating room.

Concrete details are usually stronger than atmospheric adjectives.

---

## 22. Generic Dystopian Vocabulary

AI particularly likes:

- oppressive
- bleak
- dystopian
- authoritarian
- decaying
- fractured
- unforgiving
- desolate
- grim
- sprawling

Again, these aren't forbidden words. They become a problem when the prose consists of labels rather than observations.

### Instead of

> a sprawling, decaying dystopian city

Try:

> The apartments went up thirty stories. Most of the windows were broken.

---

## 23. "Dark Underbelly"

AI loves this phrase for cities, societies, corporations, and criminal organizations.

> Beneath the city's polished exterior lies a dark underbelly...

Usually cliché.

Show the thing underneath the surface.

---

## 24. "Beneath the Veneer"

Same problem.

> Beneath the veneer of civilization...

This has become a very recognizable AI-literary phrase.

---

## 25. "Beneath the Surface"

Another favorite.

> Beneath the surface, however, something darker was happening.

Often the writing would be better if you simply described what was happening.

---

## 26. "Peeling Back the Layers"

AI loves this metaphor.

> As they peeled back the layers of the mystery...

Humans use it too, but it is heavily associated with generated analytical prose.

---

## 27. "Rich Tapestry"

Avoid unless you're intentionally writing purple prose.

> a rich tapestry of cultures and experiences

> woven into the broader tapestry of society

This is one of the clearest AI clichés.

---

## 28. "Weaves" and "Intertwines"

AI frequently describes stories and themes as fabric:

> The story weaves together...

> The narrative intertwines...

> These themes are woven throughout...

One metaphor occasionally works. Repeatedly using fabric metaphors makes prose feel generated.

---

## 29. "Journey"

AI turns everything into a journey:

> his journey through the city

> a journey of discovery

> an emotional journey

> the player's journey

Unless the character is actually traveling somewhere, consider using something more specific.

---

## 30. "Embark"

> embark on a journey

> embark on an adventure

> embark on a quest

This is particularly common in fantasy and game descriptions.

---

## 31. "Delve Into"

One of the most recognizable AI phrases.

> delve into the mysteries of...

> delve deeper into...

In fiction, just have the character investigate something.

---

## 32. "Dive Into"

Same family:

> dive into the mysteries...

> dive deeper into...

> dive into the world of...

More natural alternatives are usually simply:

> investigate  
> explore  
> examine  
> enter

---

## 33. "Step Into"

Very common in promotional fiction/game copy:

> Step into a world where...

> Step into the shoes of...

It can work in an intentionally promotional voice, but sounds generic elsewhere.

---

# PHILOSOPHICAL / THEMATIC AI

## 34. Forced Meaning

AI tends to assume everything has a thematic significance.

### AI

> The weapon isn't merely a tool of violence; it represents humanity's destructive relationship with technology.

### Human

> The weapon was ugly. Cyd kept it anyway.

Don't automatically explain the symbolism.

---

## 35. "A Reflection Of..."

> a reflection of humanity's relationship with technology

> a reflection of the character's inner turmoil

> a reflection of society's values

Sometimes correct. Frequently generic.

---

## 36. "A Testament To..."

> a testament to humanity's resilience

> a testament to the game's attention to detail

This is particularly common in generated prose.

---

## 37. "Serves As A Reminder"

> serves as a reminder of the past

> serves as a reminder of humanity's mistakes

Usually the object can simply exist.

---

## 38. "A Powerful Reminder"

Even more AI-ish.

> a powerful reminder of the consequences...

This tends to announce significance instead of creating it.

---

## 39. "Raises Questions About..."

AI frequently ends descriptions with:

> raises questions about what it means to be human.

This is especially common in science fiction.

---

## 40. "What It Means To Be Human"

One of the biggest science-fiction clichés in AI writing.

Use only if this is genuinely what the story is about.

---

## 41. "The Human Condition"

Same.

---

## 42. "Our Relationship With Technology"

Another extremely common AI sci-fi theme.

It isn't inherently bad. It is just so generic that it often tells the reader almost nothing.

---

## 43. "Explores Themes Of..."

> explores themes of identity, technology, and humanity

This is analysis, not fiction.

The story should *contain* those themes rather than announcing them.

---

## 44. "Blurring The Lines Between..."

> blurring the lines between human and machine

> blurring the lines between reality and fiction

> blurring the lines between player and character

Very common AI phrasing.

---

## 45. "At A Deeper Level"

> At a deeper level, the story is really about...

This often means the writer is about to explain something the reader was supposed to discover.

---

# CHARACTER WRITING

## 46. Over-Explaining Characters

AI often introduces characters with a complete psychological profile.

> Marcus was a deeply troubled man whose difficult childhood had shaped his distrust of authority.

A human story might simply begin:

> Marcus didn't trust cops.

Then demonstrate why.

---

## 47. "Haunted By His Past"

Extremely common AI character shorthand.

> haunted by his past

> haunted by memories

> haunted by the events of...

If the past matters, show the memory or its consequences.

---

## 48. "Carrying The Weight Of..."

> carrying the weight of his past

> carrying the weight of his responsibilities

> carrying the weight of his decisions

Cliché and frequently generated.

---

## 49. "Struggling With His Inner Demons"

Very common AI phrase.

Unless you are deliberately writing melodrama, find a specific problem.

---

## 50. "Inner Turmoil"

AI uses this instead of describing what the character is actually thinking.

---

## 51. "A Complex Character"

Never tell me a character is complex. Make them behave inconsistently.

---

## 52. "A Nuanced Character"

Same.

---

## 53. "Multifaceted Character"

Same.

These are descriptions of the author's intentions, not characterization.

---

## 54. Characters Who Always Behave Thematically

AI characters can feel like embodiments of ideas.

The cynical character always says cynical things.

The compassionate character always says compassionate things.

The mysterious character speaks in riddles.

Humans are inconsistent.

Let characters:

- contradict themselves
- misunderstand things
- say something stupid
- change their minds
- have irrelevant interests
- be funny at inappropriate times
- get distracted
- make decisions for petty reasons

---

# DIALOGUE

## 55. Everyone Speaks Perfectly

AI dialogue is often too clean.

### AI

> "I understand your concerns. However, we must consider the potential consequences of our actions."

### Human

> "Yeah, I get it. But if we do that, people die."

Perfect grammar and complete thoughts aren't inherently bad. **Everyone** speaking that way is.

---

## 56. Everyone Sounds Like The Author

This is one of the strongest AI fiction tells.

Characters with completely different backgrounds often share:

- the same sentence length
- the same vocabulary
- the same rhetorical habits
- the same politeness
- the same metaphor style

Give people different verbal habits.

---

## 57. Dialogue That Exists To Explain The Setting

### AI

> "As you know, the Coldwater Basin has been under Architect's control for thirty years."

If both characters already know this, they wouldn't say it.

### Better

> "Architect shut down the east gate again."

> "Third time this month."

Now the setting is implied.

---

## 58. Characters Saying Their Own Characterization

### AI

> "I've always been a stubborn man, and my refusal to give up is one of my defining traits."

Humans rarely narrate themselves like character sheets.

---

## 59. Characters Explaining Their Motivation

### AI

> "I did this because I was afraid of losing you."

Sometimes appropriate, but don't make every emotional motivation explicit.

---

## 60. Excessive "I see"

AI dialogue often uses:

> "I see."

> "I understand."

> "Indeed."

> "Interesting."

> "Fascinating."

These are especially suspicious when characters respond to information merely to acknowledge that it was received.

---

## 61. Everyone Is Articulate During Crisis

Real people forget words.

They swear.

They repeat themselves.

They say:

> "What?"

> "No."

> "Wait."

> "Fuck. Fuck."

AI tends to produce polished speeches while someone is being eaten.

---

## 62. Everyone Has A Quip

AI-generated fiction sometimes makes every character witty.

If everyone is funny, nobody is funny.

---

# NARRATIVE STRUCTURE

## 63. The Perfect Paragraph

AI often constructs paragraphs like:

1. topic sentence
2. explanation
3. example
4. consequence
5. thematic conclusion

Fiction doesn't need this structure.

A paragraph can simply be:

> The dog was eating the corpse.

And then move on.

---

## 64. Excessive Exposition

AI tends to explain everything the moment it becomes relevant.

Human stories often let information accumulate.

Instead of:

> Architect was a powerful artificial intelligence that had achieved singularity and subsequently...

You can have someone discover:

> The old terminal still had one file on it.

Then:

> ARCHITECT // ROOT ACCESS

Then much later, explain what that means.

---

## 65. Explaining Every Mystery

AI dislikes unresolved information.

But mystery requires unanswered questions.

You don't need:

> The strange noise was caused by...

Sometimes:

> Something moved behind the wall.

And that's all the reader gets.

---

## 66. Explaining Foreshadowing

Never:

> This would later prove to be an important moment.

Just make it important later.

---

## 67. "Little Did He Know"

Very cliché.

> Little did Marcus know that...

Avoid.

---

## 68. "Unbeknownst To Him"

Same.

---

## 69. "What He Didn't Know..."

Same.

These announce that the author is about to reveal dramatic irony instead of creating it naturally.

---

## 70. "Before He Knew It"

AI frequently uses:

> Before he knew it...

> Before she knew what was happening...

Often replace it with the actual sequence of events.

---

## 71. "Everything Changed"

AI loves dramatic turning-point declarations.

> Everything changed that night.

Sometimes it works. Usually the story should demonstrate the change.

---

## 72. "Nothing Would Ever Be The Same"

Extremely cliché.

---

## 73. "Little Did They Know..."

Extremely cliché.

---

# TRANSITIONS

## 74. Excessive "However"

AI uses "however" as a structural joint.

> However, the door was locked.

> However, this wasn't the end.

> However, Marcus disagreed.

Fiction often reads better if you simply write the next thing.

---

## 75. "That Said"

> That said, there was another problem.

Often unnecessary.

---

## 76. "Nevertheless"

Formal AI transition.

---

## 77. "Nonetheless"

Same.

---

## 78. "Furthermore"

Almost never belongs in ordinary narrative prose.

---

## 79. "Moreover"

Usually sounds like an essay.

---

## 80. "Additionally"

Same.

---

## 81. "As Such"

AI favorite:

> As such, they were forced to leave.

Usually:

> So they left.

---

## 82. "With That In Mind"

Essay language.

---

## 83. "Against This Backdrop"

Very AI literary-analysis language.

---

# ABSTRACT LANGUAGE

## 84. Too Many Abstract Nouns

AI loves:

- experience
- significance
- complexity
- atmosphere
- dynamic
- interaction
- environment
- relationship
- perspective
- development
- progression
- exploration
- identity
- humanity

Abstract nouns are useful, but too many make fiction feel like an essay.

### Instead of

> The atmosphere of fear was intensified by the oppressive environment.

Try:

> Nobody talked in the elevator.

---

## 85. Nominalization

Turning verbs into nouns makes prose feel bureaucratic.

### AI

> The implementation of the system resulted in an improvement of communication.

### Human

> The system improved communication.

### AI fiction

> His observation of the creature led to the realization of its hostility.

### Human

> He watched the creature. It bared its teeth.

---

## 86. "In Terms Of"

Usually filler.

> In terms of atmosphere, the city is...

Just describe the atmosphere.

---

## 87. "When It Comes To"

Same.

> When it comes to violence...

Just:

> Violence...

---

## 88. "In The Context Of"

Academic language.

---

## 89. "With Regard To"

Academic/corporate language.

---

# AI'S FAVORITE INTENSIFIERS

## 90. "Deeply"

> deeply unsettling

> deeply disturbing

> deeply meaningful

> deeply human

AI uses "deeply" to make ordinary adjectives sound profound.

---

## 91. "Truly"

> truly unique

> truly remarkable

> truly immersive

> truly terrifying

Usually remove it.

---

## 92. "Genuinely"

> genuinely strange

> genuinely disturbing

> genuinely impressive

AI sometimes uses this to imitate sincerity.

---

## 93. "Very"

AI often uses generic intensifiers instead of finding a better word.

---

## 94. "Really"

Particularly common when AI tries to sound conversational:

> really strange

> really interesting

> really important

---

## 95. "Quite"

> quite unusual

> quite remarkable

> quite unsettling

Can give prose an artificial British/academic quality.

---

# METAPHOR PROBLEMS

## 96. Too Many Metaphors

AI may write:

> The city was a machine, its streets the veins and its towers the bones of some enormous mechanical beast.

One metaphor can be effective. Three in one sentence starts feeling manufactured.

---

## 97. Metaphors That Explain Themselves

### AI

> The city was a cage, trapping its inhabitants both physically and psychologically.

The second half explains the first.

### Better

> The city was a cage.

Then show the cage.

---

## 98. Mixed Metaphors

AI can generate metaphors that sound individually impressive but don't belong together.

> The city was a sleeping beast, its streets a maze of veins, grinding toward an uncertain horizon.

Pick one image.

---

## 99. Metaphorical Inflation

AI often takes a small event and makes it cosmic.

> His decision sent ripples through the very fabric of society.

Maybe he just quit his job.

---

# SPECIFICITY

## 100. Generic Description Instead Of Specific Description

AI:

> The room was old and rundown.

Human:

> The carpet had turned brown around the toilet.

Specificity feels authored.

---

## 101. Generic Names For Things

AI often reaches for:

> the mysterious figure

> the strange device

> the old building

> the imposing structure

Give the reader an actual detail.

> The man had a yellow raincoat and one shoe.

---

## 102. Adjective Before Noun Instead Of Concrete Detail

AI:

> a massive, imposing building

Human:

> The building blocked the afternoon sun.

The second tells you what "massive" means.

---

# REPETITION

## 103. Repeating The Premise

AI often restates information:

> Architect controlled the city.

Later:

> The city's residents lived under Architect's control.

Later:

> Life under Architect was...

Humans generally trust readers to remember.

---

## 104. Repeating The Same Adjective

If something is:

> eerie

then:

> eerie

then:

> eerie

AI often does this because it is maintaining a semantic description.

Change the description or stop describing it.

---

## 105. Repeating The Same Sentence Structure

> He walked into the room. He looked at the desk. He picked up the gun. He walked toward the door.

Vary rhythm.

> He walked in.

The gun was on the desk.

He picked it up and left.

---

## 106. Pronoun Avoidance

AI sometimes repeats names unnaturally:

> Marcus looked at Sarah. Marcus noticed that Sarah was crying. Marcus walked toward Sarah.

Human:

> Marcus looked at Sarah. She was crying. He walked over.

---

# PURPLE PROSE

## 107. Every Moment Is Important

AI tends to make ordinary events sound significant.

> She opened the refrigerator, marking the beginning of a journey that would change everything.

Sometimes opening a refrigerator is just opening a refrigerator.

---

## 108. Every Object Has Symbolism

> The broken clock symbolized the fractured nature of his relationship with his father.

Maybe it is just a broken clock.

---

## 109. Every Scene Has A "Sense Of"

AI loves:

> a sense of danger

> a sense of wonder

> a sense of isolation

> a sense of tension

> a sense of foreboding

Show the source of the feeling.

---

## 110. "Evokes"

> The empty street evokes a sense of loneliness.

Try:

> The street was empty except for a shopping cart rolling in circles.

---

## 111. "Conveys"

> His expression conveys his grief.

Again, show it.

---

## 112. "Highlights"

> This highlights the contrast between...

This is analytical language sneaking into fiction.

---

## 113. "Underscores"

> This underscores the importance of...

Very AI literary-analysis language.

---

## 114. "Reflects"

> This reflects the broader themes of...

Again: analysis rather than storytelling.

---

# THE "TOO HUMAN" AI PROBLEM

Ironically, AI can sound fake when it tries too hard to sound casual.

## 132. "Here's The Thing"

> Here's the thing: the city isn't actually abandoned.

Can work. Repeatedly, it sounds like a generated YouTube script.

---

## 133. "But Here's The Catch"

AI loves announcing twists.

> But here's the catch...

Just give us the catch.

---

## 134. "And That's The Point"

AI often ends paragraphs with:

> And that's the point.

> That's exactly the point.

Usually the reader already understands.

---

## 135. "The Thing Is"

Same.

---

## 136. "Of Course"

AI uses "of course" to simulate conversational familiarity.

> Of course, there was a problem.

If the fact is obvious, just state it.

---

## 137. "Needless To Say"

Usually needless.

---

## 138. "Simply Put"

AI announces that it is simplifying something.

Just simplify it.

---

## 139. "In Other Words"

Often indicates unnecessary repetition.

# NARRATOR PROBLEMS

## 140. The Narrator Constantly Explains The Reader's Reaction

> It was a shocking sight.

> The scene was deeply disturbing.

> The reader might find this unsettling.

The last one is particularly artificial.

Don't tell the reader what they feel.

---

## 141. The Narrator Constantly Explains Importance

> This would prove to be important later.

> This moment would become significant.

> It was a pivotal moment.

Let later events establish importance.

---

## 142. The Narrator Constantly Explains Themes

> This was a metaphor for...

> This represented...

> This reflected...

If your fiction requires a narrator to explain its themes, the scene may need more work.

---

# RHYTHM

## 143. Every Sentence Is Medium Length

AI often produces:

> medium sentence. medium sentence. medium sentence. medium sentence.

Human writing has more variation.

> It stopped.

The machine made one last clicking sound, somewhere deep inside the wall, and then everything went quiet.

Nobody moved.

---

## 144. Every Paragraph Is Similar Length

AI often produces suspiciously uniform paragraphs.

Human prose can have:

> a huge paragraph

> a one-line paragraph

> a fragment

> another huge paragraph

Rhythm matters.

---

## 145. No Fragments

AI grammar correction often removes useful fragments.

Human:

> Pretty bad.

> Not today.

> Three hours later.

> Fuck.

These can give prose a natural rhythm.

---

## 146. Too Much Complete Grammar

Real internal monologue and dialogue aren't always grammatically complete.

> Need coffee.

> Can't do this.

> What the hell?

Not:

> I am currently experiencing a significant need for coffee.

---

# AI DIALOGUE RHYTHM

## 147. Nobody Interrupts Anyone

AI dialogue often looks like:

> "I think we should leave."

> "I disagree. We should stay."

> "Why?"

> "Because the storm is approaching."

Real conversations overlap, derail, misunderstand, and interrupt.

---

## 148. Everyone Answers The Question

Human dialogue often doesn't.

> "Where were you?"

> "Does it matter?"

> "Yes."

> "Then you already know."

AI tends to answer directly because it is optimizing for clarity.

---

## 149. Everyone Says Exactly What They Mean

Characters should lie, evade, misunderstand, posture, joke, and change the subject.

---

## 150. Dialogue That Exists To Transfer Information

If a character says something only because the reader needs to know it, the dialogue will often sound fake.

Give the characters their own reason for saying it.

# THE BIGGEST FICTION-WRITING RULE

## 165. Show More, Explain Less

This doesn't mean "never explain."

It means **don't describe the meaning of something immediately after describing the thing itself.**

### AI

> The corridor was dark and oppressive, creating a deep sense of isolation. The silence was a stark reminder of how completely the building had been abandoned.

### Better

> The corridor lights had been removed.

> Every door stood open.

> Somewhere above him, a toilet flushed.

The second version gives the reader things to interpret.

---

# THE SECOND BIGGEST RULE

## 166. Replace Abstract Claims With Physical Facts

### Instead of

> He was deeply afraid.

Try:

> He couldn't get his key into the lock.

### Instead of

> The city was oppressive.

Try:

> The loudspeakers started at six every morning.

### Instead of

> The creature was horrifying.

Try:

> It had a human hand where its jaw should have been.

### Instead of

> The room felt lonely.

Try:

> There were twelve chairs. One had been pulled away from the table.

---

# THE THIRD BIGGEST RULE

## 167. Stop Trying To Sound Profound

AI frequently reaches for significance.

Human writers often just notice something weird.

### AI

> The abandoned children's toys served as a haunting reminder of the innocence that had been lost.

### Human

> There were toys in the hallway.

> Most of them had their batteries removed.

The second is less "writerly" and potentially much creepier.

---

# THE FOURTH BIGGEST RULE

## 168. Let Characters Be Stupid

AI characters are often unnaturally competent.

Humans:

- forget things
- misunderstand
- make bad assumptions
- panic
- lie badly
- lose arguments
- say the wrong thing
- misremember
- make decisions for petty reasons

Imperfection makes characters feel real.

---

# THE FIFTH BIGGEST RULE

## 169. Let Characters Be Inconsistent

AI often keeps characters psychologically consistent with their descriptions.

Humans aren't.

A miser can be generous once.

A coward can be brave once.

A cruel person can love their dog.

A genius can make an incredibly stupid mistake.

Contradiction is human.

---

# THE SIXTH BIGGEST RULE

## 170. Don't Make Everything Symmetrical

AI likes:

> X, Y, and Z.

> both X and Y.

> not X but Y.

> X yet Y.

> on one hand / on the other.

> problem / solution.

> setup / explanation / conclusion.

Break the pattern.

---

# THE SEVENTH BIGGEST RULE

## 171. Use The Weird Detail

Generic:

> The old man was unsettling.

Specific:

> He kept six dead pigeons in his freezer and got angry when anyone called them dead.

AI tends toward the first.

Human writers are often remembered for the second.

# A Practical "Humanize This" Checklist

Before considering a piece of AI-assisted fiction finished, ask:

### Language

- [ ] Are there too many em dashes?
- [ ] Are there adjective triplets?
- [ ] Are there repeated three-item lists?
- [ ] Are there too many abstract nouns?
- [ ] Are there redundant adjective pairs?
- [ ] Are there unnecessary intensifiers?
- [ ] Are there corporate/essay words in narrative prose?
- [ ] Are sentences suspiciously grammatically perfect?

### Description

- [ ] Am I describing concrete things?
- [ ] Am I using specific details rather than generic adjectives?
- [ ] Am I explaining what an image means?
- [ ] Am I telling the reader how to feel?
- [ ] Does every object have symbolism?
- [ ] Does every scene need to be profound?

### Characters

- [ ] Do characters have different voices?
- [ ] Do they interrupt each other?
- [ ] Do they misunderstand things?
- [ ] Do they sometimes say the wrong thing?
- [ ] Do they have petty or irrational motivations?
- [ ] Do they contradict their established personalities?
- [ ] Are they allowed to be stupid?

### Dialogue

- [ ] Does everyone sound like the narrator?
- [ ] Does everyone speak in complete sentences?
- [ ] Does every question get a direct answer?
- [ ] Are characters explaining things they already know?
- [ ] Is dialogue being used as an exposition dump?
- [ ] Does anyone actually evade, lie, or change the subject?

### Structure

- [ ] Does every paragraph have a neat conclusion?
- [ ] Does every mystery get explained?
- [ ] Does every scene announce its significance?
- [ ] Am I foreshadowing by saying "little did he know"?
- [ ] Am I repeating information the reader already knows?
- [ ] Are paragraphs and sentences too uniform?

### Most importantly

- [ ] Did I leave anything unexplained?
- [ ] Did I allow the reader to infer something?
- [ ] Did I include a strange, specific detail?
- [ ] Did I let something happen without explaining why it matters?
- [ ] Does the prose occasionally get messy?
- [ ] Does it sound like someone with a particular voice wrote it?

# The Core Principle

The easiest way to make AI fiction sound human is not to replace every suspicious word.

It's to change the **relationship between the prose and the reader**.

AI tends to say:

> **Here is what happened. Here is what it means. Here is why it matters. Here is how you should feel about it.**

Human fiction is more comfortable saying:

> **Here is what happened.**

And then moving on.

Trust the reader.

Be specific.

Let characters be idiots.

Let sentences be uneven.

Let jokes sit there without explanations.

Let mysteries stay mysterious.

Let something be weird without telling us that it's weird.

And, occasionally, just write:

> Fuck.
