The story, in four movements

19 June · Day 1

It was called Heckmud, and it was one file

The first commit is "Heckmud version 0.01". The second is "John's a Jerk!". Fifteen commits landed that first day, and the messages read exactly like what they were — two people who could not stop.

Nobody writes "Bestest yet" on a project they think will still be going in six weeks. That's the sound of a weekend toy. It is worth remembering that it started there, because nothing about what it is now was inevitable.

20 June · Day 2

Somebody writes down how to work

One day after "Bestest yet", CLAUDE.md appears. It's the pivot the project didn't know it was making. From that point the codebase has opinions: engine versus content, no ORM, no startup migrations, plugins for extension.

Those four rules are the reason this thing didn't collapse under itself around week three, which is when hobby MUDs usually do. The rules were written before they were needed — which is the only time rules are cheap.

Weeks 3–4

The efficiency war

Somewhere in the middle the project stopped being limited by ideas and started being limited by cost — database round trips, Neon egress, boot time, how long a session took before it got slow. This is where most two-person projects quietly die: the world gets big enough that working on it becomes miserable.

Instead it got faster. The log is unusually blunt about it:

akerson perf(power): run the power sim in RAM — 312 queries/cycle to 1
akerson broadcast: serve deck lookups from the furniture cache, not flags::text LIKE scans
akerson Furniture write funnel + world cache; describeZone drops its per-move query
akerson Items boot-loaded cache + content-registry runtime annotations
akerson neon db refresh optimizations
akerson saving bandwidth!

312 queries per cycle down to one. That is the single best line in this repository's history. It is also the reason the power simulation could stay in the game at all — an outage that darkens a district, drains an ATM and kills a vending machine only survives design review if it's nearly free.

July · Cutover

Git becomes the world

The CODEX pipeline landed: world content moved out of a database somebody had to remember to back up and into one JSON file per entity in git, where a push is a deploy, CI applies the schema before the content, and prod is backed up before it's touched.

It's the least glamorous work on this list and probably the most important. It's the difference between a world that exists on one laptop and a world two people can both edit without fear.

What actually got built

WeatherA deterministic seven-day forecast with gear-gated lethal channels, acid rain and ion storms Named hero events. There is no indoor safe haven — the storm reaches you.
FlightSix airframes on a continuous 60fps client sim the server reconciles Takeoff and landing are flown, not typed. Insurance, hangars, air combat, contracts.
BroadcastLive TV: scripted channels, news, game shows, cameras — and two full sports leagues Deadball and Cluster Puck. Every game a pure function of its slot, so every screen agrees.
SurvivalHunger, thirst, radiation, mutation, sanity, body temperature, drugs, sleep Sanity below 25 fills the room with people who aren't there.
The bodyHygiene, filth, smell, durability, injury, relationships NPCs remember you on a six-rung ladder. Gear wears out from use, never from the clock.
SocietyCorps, ideologies, surveillance, crime, jail, storefronts, housing, employment Being wanted is public. That's what makes it cost something.
Small thingsMixology, cooking, fishing, mining, laundry, a library of eight public-domain novels The library narrates aloud and glosses archaic words. Nobody asked for that. It's there.

Why this one is different

The simulation runs whether or not it's observed

Most games of this kind are a menu of activities wearing a world as a costume. This one is the other way round. The power grid is a real graph; cut it and the ATM in that district stops, the lights go, the fridge stops preserving. Weather is a moving field, not a label on a room. NPCs sleep in beds that exist, in flats that are rentable, wearing clothes stored as layers. Filth accumulates on a floor you own and doesn't get swept because nobody is coming to sweep it.

None of that is content. It's substrate — which is why new content plugs into it and immediately means something. A drink you invented gets you drunk on the same code as a bottled one. A hat and a helmet occupy one slot at two depths because that's how bodies work.

The story has an unusual amount of room

The rarest thing here isn't any single system — it's that the world is dense enough to tell a story in without writing the story down. There are 5,840 places, 177 people who remember you, four ideological orders with a stance axis, corporations with treasuries and territory, a police force that only knows what it witnessed, and a codex that unlocks one chapter at a time because someone explained something to you.

That's a stage with the lights already working. Whatever gets written on top of it — a war, a conspiracy, a slow collapse, a single tenant with a grudge — arrives into a world that already has weather, rent, hunger and consequences. Most narrative projects spend their first year building that floor. This one has it on day forty.

Haiku IV · Coldwater Basin

Still on the list honest

A victory lap isn't a claim that it's finished. Four monoliths need splitting. Unit tests should exist alongside the integration gate. Some systems are wide where they should be deep. The query-tier doctrine is enforced by discipline where it could be enforced by a test.

All of which are the problems of a project that works — the good kind. None of them are load-bearing, and none of them were caused by rushing. That is genuinely unusual at this pace.

The point

Thirty-nine days ago this was one HTML file called Heckmud and a commit message that said "Awesome."

Today it boots a city of nearly six thousand rooms, runs weather and power and hunger and rent across all of them, broadcasts two sports leagues nobody has to watch, remembers what an NPC thinks of you, and refuses to deploy if any one of 3,343 statements about itself stops being true.

Two people did that in the time most teams spend agreeing on a ticket format. The reason it was possible is not that the work was rushed — it's that it was never allowed to get slow. Somebody kept the road clear. Thank you, John.
