Same play, two renderers

Both screens below are drawn from one shared simulation: the same ten skaters, two goalies, puck, check and fight, stepped together at 120 Hz. Only the drawing differs. Use the slow-motion setting to compare how each one handles turning, the hit on the boards and the fight cut-in.

A

2D illustrated sprites

NHL '94–'96 lineage–

What to watch: facing snaps between eight directions and the stride runs a fixed six-frame cycle, the way a sprite sheet does. The man hit into the boards plays a canned stagger and a canned lying frame, so where he hits can't change how he falls. The fight is a separate side-view screen with its own backdrop. Turning a crisp flat look into this is cheap; every new move is new frames times eight directions.

B

3D bodies, broadcast camera

NHL '97–'99 lineage–

What to watch: turning, stride, lean and arm swing are continuous. The check turns the victim into a ragdoll, so his speed and the point he meets the glass decide how he folds and where he lands, and the glass flexes and cracks at that spot. The camera pans and zooms with the play, and the fight close-up is the same scene with the camera moved down to the ice, so the blood, the dropped gloves and the crowd stay where they are. Players reflect in the ice.

My call: B, the 3D renderer. The things you asked for are the things it does and sprites don't: checks that land differently every time, a fight that cuts in without leaving the arena, injuries that stay on the ice, and a camera that can move. I'd borrow A's dark outlines so the men still read on the tablet.

Both are prototypes drawn in code with no art assets, running a hand-scripted sequence rather than the real sim. The production build would plug the chosen renderer into the existing broadcast payloads.
