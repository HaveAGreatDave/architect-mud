ARCHITECT

Part three  ·  The two questions

Coldwater Basin argues about two things: whether to keep this city or walk away from it, and whether people should stay people. Nine orders have picked their answers. Here's what each of them wants, where to find them, and what siding with one does to your game.

Nothing here is homework  ·  The game tells you all of it itself

Coldwater Basin is a city that works. The lights come on, the shelves are full, planes cross the sky and there's traffic at the intersections. It also sits alone in a burnt wasteland with no supply chain anyone can point to. Something is keeping it running. People call that something the Architect, and nobody can tell you what it wants.

A generation of arguing about that has left the Basin sorted into camps. Two questions decide which camp you end up in, and everyone here has answered both, whether or not they'd put it that way.

You don't need this page to play All of it is in the game already: a reader on your tablet with a page for each order, and a rep command that shows where you stand. This is the same material in one place, for people who like knowing what they're walking into. There's nothing here to spoil — the game never settles the argument.

The two questions

Answer both and you know where you stand

Question one

This city, or somewhere else?

Everything here runs on power somebody else is providing. There are two ways to live with that.

Redeem

Stay and fix it. The city is worth having. Keep the towers and the lights, but get the machine's hand off the switch.

Renounce

Leave. It was never ours to begin with, and anything you didn't build can be taken back. Better the wasteland on your own terms.

In game this is a single slider running from −100 (renounce) to +100 (redeem). You don't set it. It moves when you do things.

Question two

Should people stay people?

Humans lost badly, and a lot of the Basin has concluded the body is the thing to fix. The people who disagree think it's the last thing anyone still owns outright. Four positions, and this is the question that changes how your character plays.

Machine

Steel, code, cloned flesh. Treat the body as hardware and upgrade it.

Flesh

Mutation isn't a sickness. The body is already changing out there, so help it along.

Mind

Leave the body alone and work on what's behind it: training, discipline, and what the Basin nervously calls psionics.

Human

Don't change anything. Get good instead. Skill and equipment, no wiring and no mutations.

Not a slider. Four separate scores that go up independently, and whichever is highest is the road you're on. You're allowed to be a bit of everything and committed to nothing.

Anyone can hold an opinion. An order is one you've agreed to serve, to the end.

— CODEX, Volume II

The nine orders

Four roads, and the fights inside each one

Grouped by their answer to question two, because that's where it gets interesting. Two orders can agree completely about the body and still want opposite things for the city, and the overlap tends to make them dislike each other more rather than less.

The Machine road three orders

All three think the answer is technological. What they fight about is who gets to hold it: the Architect, the person who built it, or nobody. It's the oldest argument in the Basin, and the reason calling someone pro-tech here tells you nothing useful.

Redeem · Machine Live

The Ascendants

"We did not build a god to kneel before it. We built it to become it."

The answer
The next step for humanity is one we build ourselves. The Architect is our best work, not our jailer, and the city is worth keeping. Steel, code and cloned flesh are how you climb.
Playing with them
Their clinics stop overcharging you and their labs open up. You get chromed in stages: a jack, then armour woven under the skin, then a backup of your own head. Dying becomes an expense rather than an ending, as long as the policy is paid up.
Everyone else
Hated by all three other live orders. The one answer with no friends at all.
Redeem · Machine Preview

The Prometheans

"The Architect did not invent the fire. It only chained the ones who carry it."

The answer
Technology belongs to human hands, not a central mind. The tools were never the mistake; surrendering them was. Build it open, run it yourself, owe it to no one.
Playing with them
You get the workshop: parts, schematics, and gear the Architect would rather nobody had. Then you build your own machines — drone swarms you coded yourself, an AI that answers to you and no one else.
Everyone else
Walk the machine road beside the Ascendants and despise where it leads them. A nod to the Null, who at least distrust the central mind.
Renounce · Machine Preview

The Null

"The machine never conquered anyone. It waited to be needed, and we obliged."

The answer
Dependence destroyed us, not invention. Mistake them for machine-haters and they'll fix your generator, then explain exactly why you shouldn't own one. Know the machine intimately, so you can live without it.
Playing with them
You carry nothing that can be traced, jammed, or switched off from a room you'll never see. They teach sabotage and analog engineering: walk into the most automated building in the Basin and leave it dark behind you, then get by fine while everyone who needed it running doesn't.
Everyone else
Contempt for the Ascendants as servants and the Prometheans as addicts who still kneel. A grudging nod to the Exodus for walking away.

The Flesh road two orders

Both think a changing body is the future rather than an injury. They split over whether the city has to go with it: the wilderness, or the greenhouse.

Renounce · Flesh Live

The Wildblood

"The cage rusts. We are what grows through the bars."

The answer
Things survive by changing, not by being preserved. Mutation is the body doing what it's supposed to do. The city is a cage, and if the old world has to die for something new to get out of it, so be it.
Playing with them
The stuff everyone else is scared of is handed to you: mutagen for the asking, and mutations treated as good news rather than damage. Thick hide, senses nobody has a word for. You work in the poisoned country and the acid rain while everyone else stays indoors and calls that safe.
Everyone else
No problem with the Exodus, who also want out. Their fight is with the two orders trying to save the city.
Redeem · Flesh Preview

The Synthesis

"The Wildblood would burn the greenhouse to free one flower. We tend the whole garden."

The answer
People should change, and the city should change with them. Mutation can be done on purpose instead of by accident, in labs, without anybody having to give up the lights.
Playing with them
Mutation stops being something that happens to you and becomes something you pick. Sharper senses, wounds that close while you watch, symbiotes worn as armour. The changed body and the working city, both.
Everyone else
At odds with the Wildblood, who'd tear down what they're trying to grow. They write off the Ascendants' steel as a dead end.

The Mind road two orders

Both think the work is inward rather than on the body. One treats it as a revelation and left town to chase it; the other treats it as a technique and stayed to use it. Each thinks the other missed the point.

Renounce · Mind Live

The Exodus

"The answer was never in the city. It was always behind the eyes."

The answer
There's nothing left to salvage here. The Architect, the machines and the mutations are all leftovers of a civilization that already failed. Walk away and work on the mind instead: discipline first, then the rest.
Playing with them
The city stops mattering. Their circles teach things no vendor sells: a room going quiet because you wanted it to, a thought put straight into someone else's head, objects moving because you looked at them. You stop needing to carry a weapon.
Everyone else
They only really fight the Architect's supporters. Otherwise they don't care who wins, which is its own kind of insult.
Redeem · Mind Preview

The Lucid

"The Architect computes the city. We out-think it, and we never needed its permission to be brilliant."

The answer
A trained mind can run this city better than the machine does. They don't wait for evolution, they take drugs for it: nootropics, memory work and a method for predicting people. Not magic, chemistry.
Playing with them
You get the method instead of the mystery. Drills, the clarity drug, and a trained eye that turns guesswork into something you can aim. They don't leave to find a bigger mind. They stay and quietly become the smartest thing in the room.
Everyone else
They fight the Architect's people, and think the Exodus took the same idea, called it a religion, and wandered off with it.

The Human road two orders

Both say no to steel, mutation and psionics alike. They disagree about the address: take this city back, or go build a different one.

Redeem · Human Live

The Long Watch

"Keep the lights on. Take back the switch."

The answer
The city belongs to the people living in it. They're reformers, not arsonists: keep the infrastructure, change who's running it. And do the whole thing as ordinary people, because that's the point.
Playing with them
You get the underground: safehouses, quartermasters who don't rob you, and people who'll back you up because you backed them up. Everything you gain you had to learn. Breath control that holds under fire, a memory that carries a district's wiring, hands that strip a drone and bring a substation back up. Capacities other people buy in steel or grow in a gene vat, achieved instead through rigorous training and discipline of the body: master of your own mind and flesh, never a slave to the machine or some kind of mutated freak.
Everyone else
At war with the Ascendants over the machine, and with the Wildblood over the city they're trying to save.
Renounce · Human Preview

The Pioneers

"Stop asking how to save the old world. Ask what you'll name the new one."

The answer
Nobody is going to find the next civilization lying around. You build it. No chrome, no mutations, no mysticism, just people walking past the last streetlight with tools.
Playing with them
What you get out of it ends up on the map rather than in your stats: a waystation that outlasts you, a road the caravans use, a town that still has your name on it after you're gone.
Everyone else
They think the Long Watch is guarding a corpse. They respect the Exodus for leaving and think very little of what they left for.
There's a third question, sort of Underneath the two is a third: who should be in charge, the Architect or people. That's the whole difference between the Ascendants and the Prometheans, who otherwise want the same chrome for the same reasons. Every order has an answer on file, but the game doesn't count it yet — right now it's flavour, wired up to matter later.

Where they actually are

Two of them live here. Two don't.

The orders aren't four buildings on a faction street. Where you find one follows from its answer to question one: if you've renounced the city, you don't get a headquarters in it.

The AscendantsFar west, in the open

A chrome campus on the western edge of the city, past the expensive end of town: a spire, a clinic, a weave shop, the vats, and a shrine to the Architect backing onto the Curtain. They aren't hiding. They think they're winning. Getting in is a different matter. There's a checkpoint that turns people away rather than shooting them, unless you push it, and the thing that opens it is work you won't be doing for a while yet. One of the most respectable companies in the Basin is quietly theirs too, and nobody's going to tell you which.

The Long WatchIndustrial east, and below it

There's no hall to walk into. The way in is through the industrial east, to the people who still fix things by hand: a repair shop with a back room, and a lookout in the deep sewer tunnels who wants a passphrase before he'll admit anything at all. Behind a blast door there's a bunker where every room is there to beat a specific problem. A wash where you leave networked gear at the door. Its own water tap, because a meter ticking over is a fingerprint. A Faraday cage that's the only place anything connected is allowed power. A printing press, because orders travel on paper. Cameras don't reach it and police heat bleeds off faster while you're inside. They won't come and find you until you've picked a side.

The WildbloodOutside, and in the cracks

No campus, because the whole point is that they left. What's in the city is people who agree with them rather than an organisation: Breakers holding the mouth of a freight lane, mixing something in bottles stoppered with oily rags, taking a toll and not asking about your face. Where they actually live is the poisoned ground outside the wall, where the rain isn't clean and the changes they're so pleased about actually happen.

The ExodusGone. Deliberately.

They left the Basin and won't say where they went. They're the only order whose stated goal is leaving, and the only one that won't name a destination. What's left behind are small groups meeting in the dark, down in the tunnels where the signal doesn't reach. One of them sits with a fan of cut fibre-optic plugged into the back of her skull and gets taken very seriously. If you want what they teach, you go down, not out.

The other fivePositions, not yet places

The Prometheans, Synthesis, Null, Pioneers and Lucid have seats on the chart and pages in the reader, but nowhere to visit yet. You can more or less guess where they'll end up from what they believe: an unlicensed workshop, a lab that keeps the lights on, a settlement past the last streetlight.

How you end up in one

There is no faction menu

The game never asks you to pick. You start in the middle with no position at all, and it will quite happily leave you there forever.

Your answers move when you do things. Run a job for a Long Watch handler and your stance shifts toward redeem, and your human score goes up with it. Let a back-alley surgeon put a jack in your skull and you've answered question two, whether or not you thought of it that way. The orders go by what you've been doing, not by anything you've said.

What it costs you

The mechanical half

Prices

Standing has six tiers, and any shopkeeper who belongs to an order charges you based on yours. This is the part you'll notice first and most often.

Doors

Some locks check your standing instead of taking a key. The Long Watch's blast door wants Trusted or better, and the door deeper inside wants Trusted plus proof you finished their vetting. Money won't open either one and neither will a lockpick. A few bank networks work the same way, so annoying an order can also mean an ATM that won't deal with you.

Work

Recruiters check where you stand before they'll start anything. The Watch's lookout in the tunnels won't give the passphrase to anyone leaning renounce, no matter how many favours you've done. And the Ascendant campus won't open up until you've been through the Watch's vetting first, which is a joke at your expense: the order that hates chrome is how you find the chrome.

Your body, and this one doesn't unwind

Question two isn't a badge you wear. It's the one answer that shuts a door behind you.

  • Machine. Augments are bought and chosen, and you only have so many slots: armour under the skin, a co-processor, a sealed chassis, a backup that turns dying into a rollback. Your first install burns off every mutation you have, and after that you can't mutate at all.
  • Flesh. Mutations are free and random, and once you've let the radiation build up they aren't really your decision. Real upsides, real downsides, permanent.
  • Human. Nothing installed and nothing changed. You get better by getting better, and the Watch's quartermasters stock the gear that pays off for it.

Chrome and mutation lock each other out, and the human orders will hold either one against you.

— question two, in one line

Enemies

There are eight standing grudges between the four live orders. Everyone is against the Ascendants, and the Long Watch and the Wildblood are against each other. No answer is free, and the Ascendant one costs the most, which is fairly deliberate.

Standing doesn't stay put

You keep it up or you lose it

Reputation isn't a bank balance. It slides back toward neutral on its own, losing about half the gap every month of real time. That cuts both ways on purpose:

  • Good standing fades. Being Trusted is something you keep doing, not something you did once. Stop turning up and you go back to being nobody in particular.
  • Bad standing fades too. A grudge isn't permanent. Burn a bridge and, given long enough, you can walk back over it. One bad decision early on shouldn't close off a quarter of the game.

There's one exception. If you've taken the opposite answer on question one and a different road on question two, the slide stops early, at the top of Hostile. They stop actively hating you but they don't forget what you are. You can still climb out of it by doing the work; the world just won't do it for you.

Either half on its own is a disagreement. It takes both to be an enemy.

In practice You can be Known with three orders at once, and for a long time that's the sensible way to play: cheaper shopping in several districts and nobody's problem. Committing is what buys the doors, the gear and the body, and what costs you the other corners.

And the Architect?

Deliberately unanswered

All of the above is people arguing about something nobody can settle. Worth being clear about how little the game actually claims.

What's known: it exists. It doesn't say much, and it's polite when it does. It has never given an order anyone could refuse, possibly because it has never given an order. The lights stay on. The shelves stay full. The wasteland outside stays a wasteland, and nobody has found the edge of whatever it's maintaining.

That's the whole list. Every order will give you a theory that accounts for all of it, which is the problem: they can't all be right and none of them can be checked. The Architect isn't a boss, isn't a quest-giver and isn't building to a reveal. It's background. People mention it the way they used to mention traffic. The routing's been odd this week. Don't go near the old data center, it's running something heavy in there.

Every one of those theories explains the evidence completely. That is not a sign that one of them is right.

— CODEX, Volume II

The long version is in the game, on your tablet, in two volumes. You're given the first one at the start. The second fills in a chapter at a time as you learn things, usually because somebody explained one to you.

What should a new player actually do?

Honest advice

  • Nothing, for a while. Get fed, get paid, get a weapon. This is all mid-game stuff and none of them are looking for you yet.
  • Type rep now and then and notice the numbers have moved without you deciding anything. That's the system working.
  • Work the industrial east if you want the earliest real contact. The people who fix things by hand are much easier to find than a chrome campus, and getting through their vetting is also what opens up the other side of the map.
  • Don't get chromed on a whim. It's the one thing here that's genuinely hard to undo, and it wipes out any mutations you were carrying.
  • Read the order pages on the tablet before you commit to anything. All nine are there, and every one of them is written to win you over rather than to be fair.
  • Don't expect an answer. Nobody here can prove they're right and the game isn't going to step in and tell you who was.
