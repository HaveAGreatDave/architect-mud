Text-only edition of this guide — plain, linear, screen-reader first, with every diagram written out.

How it works

Reading, typing, and a city on a clock

ARCHITECT runs in a browser tab and it's built on writing: you read where you are, and you type what you do. A great deal hangs off that: a live minimap, a drag-and-drop inventory, a pocket tablet with thirty-odd apps, a television you can sit and watch, lock-picking and hacking minigames, a neon chess set, a poker table, and a long way out from all of that, a flight simulator you fly looking out of a cockpit window.

What it doesn't have is a pause button. The city keeps its own time whether you're logged in or not: a day passes every eight real hours, weather rolls across the map, streetlights come on at dusk.

You're one person in that city. You have a body with meters attached to it, an inventory, a bank balance, and skills that get better only by being used. There's no quest arrow. The game's answer to what should I do is generally whatever you can afford.

What you're looking at

The whole game is one screen: a thin strip of readouts down the left, buttons along the bottom, and the middle split into two panes.

The top pane is the room, and it keeps itself current. Walk somewhere and it repaints with the new room; someone else walks out and it repaints without them. You never have to ask for it.

The bottom pane is the log, and it scrolls. That's the running account of what happened — things said, blows landed, a door unlocking, a stranger's television bleeding through the wall. It only ever grows downward.

What that saves you Older games of this kind make you type look constantly to find out where you are. Here you don't — the room is already on screen, and it stays right. What you'll actually use is examine (or just x) to inspect a thing: x sully, x corkboard. The smartbar counts them for you — Examine (4) means four things here are worth a closer look.

Point at a region above.
  1. Your name and purse. The credits shown are the ones on your body — the losable half. Banked money isn't here.
  2. The minimap. You in the middle, rooms around you, coloured by how dangerous they are. Green edges are safe, red is not.
  3. Vitals. A live readout of the meters in section 03. Health and stamina sit here permanently; the rest appear when they start to matter.
  4. The d-pad. Lit buttons are exits that actually exist — so you can cross the entire city by clicking, without knowing a compass direction. in and out are doors.
  5. Environment. City time and date, temperature, weather, and how well lit it is. The last line is your body reporting in — Hungry there's the game warning you before it becomes a problem.
  6. Who's online. Everyone currently in the world, not just this room. It's a small city; you'll recognise names fast.
  7. The room pane. Where you are, right now. Name, safety tag, description, exits — repainted automatically whenever anything changes, so it's never out of date. Underlined words are clickable: people, scenery, exits.
  8. The divider. Drag it. If you're exploring, give the room more space; if you're in a fight or deep in conversation, give the log more.
  9. The log. What happened, in order, scrolling down. Speech, combat, people coming and going, your own commands echoed back and their results.
  10. The command line. Where you type. Every button and link on this screen is a shortcut to something you could type here yourself.
  11. The smartbar. Context buttons for what's possible in this room — it changes as you walk. The fastest way to find out what a place lets you do, and where Examine (4) tells you how many things here reward a closer look.

How a turn works

You read the room, then type one short instruction. The answer comes back in the log; if the instruction changed the room, the pane above updates itself.

the log
> x sully
A heavy man in his sixties with a burn scar up one forearm. He has the
expression of someone who has heard your story before.

> talk sully
Sully: "You look like six credits and a bad decision. Drink?"

> north
→ You step out onto the quay. Rain, and the smell of the water.
   (the room pane above has already changed)

Commands are verb, then thing: x sully, take bottle, attack rat. Short and flat. If you type something the game doesn't recognise it says so and nothing happens, so experiment.

You don't have to type Exits, people, enemies, corpses and items are clickable right there in the room text — the underlined bits. Clicking one types the sensible command for you. Plenty of people play mostly by clicking and reach for the keyboard only when they want something specific.

Where you wake up

The vat, the tablet, the street

You start in a cloning facility, because in this city dying is a service you subscribe to. Before you're even out of the room a hatch spits a tablet at you — map, bank, messages, reference library. Open it early.

Then the door opens onto Coldwater Basin and you're on your own.

Moving

The world is a grid of rooms joined by compass directions. Every room lists its exits. You travel one room at a time.

n s e wNorth, south, east, west
u   dUp and down — stairs, ladders, lifts
in   outThrough a door — into a shop, back onto the street
mapAn overhead sketch of what's around you

Buildings are entered by walking at the door from the street outside, and left the same way. The lit arrows on the d-pad are the exits that exist, so you can navigate the entire city without knowing a single compass direction.

The five things keeping you alive

Your body, tracked in real time

These drain continuously — while you play, and while you're away.

HP

Damage taken. Zero is death — survivable, but expensive.

HUNGER

Eat. Food is cheap and everywhere.

THIRST

Drink. Drains faster than hunger.

RADIATION

Climbs outdoors and in bad places. Rots you slowly.

SANITY

Frays from violence, isolation and worse. Low sanity lies to you.

None of these will kill you in the first hour. All of them will kill you in the fifth if you ignore them. Check with stats; buy a meal before you need one.

The fixes are the obvious words: eat <thing>, drink <thing>, sleep in a bed, and a clinic for radiation. use works on most of it if you can't remember which.

Radiation is the sneaky one It doesn't hurt. It just climbs — and high enough, it hands you a permanent mutation you didn't ask for. Some mutations are good. That isn't a plan.

Money, and getting some

Credits, banks, vendors

Currency is credits (₵), held in two places: cash on your body, which anyone can take off your corpse, and a bank balance, which they can't. ATMs are scattered around the city.

balanceWhat you've got, carried and banked
deposit 200At an ATM — the safe half
withdraw 50The spendable half
shop sullySee what a vendor sells
buy beerHand over credits
sell scrapThe reverse — vendors buy what they deal in

Early income is honest work: a job board with small rotating gigs, things worth scavenging off the ground, and vendors who'll take junk off your hands. Later income is less honest. That's between you and the police.

Carrying things

Inventory, gear, wear and tear

Gear wears out with use. A coat that's taken a beating says so when you x it, and there are people who repair things for a fee. Gear at zero condition is destroyed outright — don't ride a good weapon into the ground.

Violence

Slow, decisive, and worth avoiding

Fighting resolves on a cooldown of a few seconds. There's no advantage in mashing keys, and a large advantage in choosing the fight.

Rust Alley — combat
> attack scav
You swing the pipe wrench at the scav's head — it connects with a wet crack.
The scav lunges, opening a gash across your left arm. You're bleeding.

> attack scav
Your wrench takes him down. He doesn't get up.

> loot corpse
You take: 41 credits, a chipped blade, half a ration bar.

Hits land on specific body parts, armour soaks specific damage types, and bleeding keeps costing HP after the fight ends. Bodies stay lootable by anyone for an hour of real time, including yours.

Three things you can do mid-fight

Once you're swinging, the swings happen on their own. What you decide is this:

aim headCall your shot at a body part instead of taking what you're given
powWind up. The next blow lands for 250% damage
dodgeGive up your next swing to make yourself much harder to hit

Aiming is opt-in and easy to overrate. aim head, aim left leg, aim auto to stop. A called shot costs accuracy, and at low skill it costs a great deal of it — a head shot from an unskilled hand mostly misses. Aim at a limb you want to wreck, or once you're good enough to afford it. Otherwise leave it on auto.

pow and dodge share a cooldown, so you're always choosing offence or defence, never both. pow is a wind-up, not an extra swing: it takes longer to land and hurts far more. It's how you finish something, and a bad trade when you're the one running out of HP. dodge gives up your attack for a few seconds of much better defence — for when a fight has turned and you need one exchange to go your way.

And you can leave. flee breaks off a fight you're losing, sneak keeps you out of one — each observer notices you separately, so one person spotting you doesn't mean everybody has.

Rust Alley — calling a shot
> aim head
You'll go for the head. Called shots cost accuracy.

> pow
You plant your feet and draw the wrench back.
You drive the pipe wrench into the scav's head — CRITICAL POWER.

Two kinds of ground

The city core is watched ground, not safe ground. Past the edges, in the badlands west of town and the wastes south through the Curtain, nobody is watching at all, and whatever you were carrying is theirs if they win.

The rule the city runs on Nothing here is illegal until it's seen. Bank your money, and don't carry anything out there you'd be sad to lose.

Dying

You wake up back in the vat. You keep every skill you ever earned; you lose what was on your body, which is now lying wherever you fell for anyone to pick up. Death is a setback and an errand.

Getting better at things

Twenty-six skills, no experience bar

You improve a skill by doing the thing. Picking locks makes you better at locks; missing punches makes you better at punching. Check progress with skills.

None of the cooking, the fishing, the chemistry or the flying is locked behind a level. It's locked behind having tried it a few times and been bad at it first.

Other people

Talking, and being remembered

say helloSpeak aloud to the room
me shrugsA freeform action line — me leans on the bar
talk gradyStart a conversation with an NPC
whoWho else is online

The NPCs remember you. Talk to the same barkeep enough times and he stops greeting you like a stranger; some will do things for someone they know that they'd never do for a walk-in. Rudeness persists too.

Living here

What the city is actually for

The first thing to get is somewhere to live, and what you're buying is the lock. Sleeping leaves your body lying in the room where you slept, and a body is lootable and killable whether or not anyone's home behind it.

You can't just doss anywhere, either. The game only lets you sleep in a place you rent, a tagged sanctuary, or a flat somebody left unlocked — and the last two mean anyone at all can walk in on you while you're under.

A place of your own

Vacant units advertise themselves. Stand in one and type rent. From then on it's yours: it locks, it holds your things, and sleeping in a safe room you own recovers you faster than dossing anywhere else.

rentTake the unit you're standing in
lock / unlockThe door. Use it.
upgrade lockBetter lock, harder to pick — worth it once you own anything
sleepTimed recovery, scaled by how safe the place is
deedBuy a shopfront, if you'd rather be a landlord than a tenant

Rent falls due every seven days on the city calendar, and the city clock is fast — a day is eight real hours, so a week's rent comes round in a couple of real days. Miss it and you're out. The tablet's Calendar app tracks the due date.

Your mess is now your problem Unowned rooms get swept clean nightly by somebody else. A room you own keeps its stains for a full rent cycle. That's why anybody owns a mop.

The kitchen

Cooking is a full craft, not a crafting menu. You chop, season, heat and time things, and the pan can be wrong in ways it'll tell you about.

chop / mince / cutPrep an ingredient before it goes in
marinate / cure / butterSlower preparations, better results
cookStart the pan. Needs a stove, and needs to make sense
stir / flip / tasteWork it while it cooks — the part that takes skill
plateServe it up
cookbook / recipeWhat you know; save a combination you invented
shoplistWhat you still need to buy, derived from your inventory
prepare <recipe>Walks the steps for you — and stops at the stove, because heat is the part you're supposed to do

One thing catches everyone out: dry starch needs liquid. A pan of pasta with nothing in it refuses to cook, so fill a vessel at a water source first.

The bar

A finished drink lives on the vessel. That's why a good mug is worth owning. mix at a bar, brew if there's a machine for it, and drink it where you are — anything worth finishing takes long enough that leaving the room cuts it short.

The television

Every set in the game is a real piece of furniture on the real power grid. Find one, and:

watch tvStart watching whatever's on
tune <n>Change the channel
tune 0Switch the set off — for the whole room
tv offStop watching, and leave it on for everyone else

Channels run a genuine schedule, and the programmes are assembled live rather than replayed — news, sport, talk shows, films. It's worth an evening on its own once you have somewhere to sit.

You can be on television Game shows are the one broadcast you can be inside. Stand in the studio when a round opens and you're a contestant — answer with guess, on air, citywide, for money. One asks what things are worth; the other asks what you know about the city.

The game's on

Two sports run real seasons with real standings: Deadball (baseball) on Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays, and Cluster Puck (hockey) on Tuesdays, Thursdays and Sundays. Games are simulated fresh, called by commentary, and carry a score bug on screen.

standingsThe league tables
wagerPut money on a game
worldseries / cupThe postseason, when it comes round

A bar with the set on is the room's game, not yours: sit down, order something, and let the place watch it with you.

Weather, which is trying to kill you

The forecast runs seven days ahead and you can read it on the tablet. Most of it's texture. Some of it's not.

  • Severe weather is lethal — but only to the unprepared. The right coat turns a killer into an inconvenience.
  • There's no free safe haven indoors. A blacked-out building has no heating, and cold kills inside just as well as out.
  • Wind costs stamina on every outdoor step. It never blocks you; it just bleeds you.
  • Named events happen. Acid rain. An ion storm that drops the entire city grid — lights, ATMs, televisions.
  • The warning is vague on purpose. A ⚠ severe band on the forecast tells you something is coming that week. It doesn't tell you what, or exactly when.
Take the coat Cheap, boring, and the difference between weather being scenery and weather being the thing that killed you.

Your first hour

Grady, two clonejackers, and a hole in the road

You don't have to invent something to do. There's a proper opening chain, and it's built to teach you the game by making you use it.

Find Grady

Off Ironside there's a cramped front room made of corrugated sheet and honest intentions, with flashlights and coils of spare cells hanging off every nail: Two-Cell Supply. Behind the crates that serve as a counter is Grady "Two-Cell" Okonkwo, who watches the door the way a fisherman watches a net.

He sells the boring things that keep you alive — water, jerky, bandages, batteries, lights — at prices a newborn can afford. talk grady.

The First Hour

Grady has a complaint. Clonejackers are working the strip south of the Embassy, and they're killing his business by killing his customers — people exactly like you, about an hour younger. He'd like two of them gone, and his stolen flashlight cells back.

  1. Head south past the Embassy's warm lights. Quests draw a green GPS line on your map — follow it, or hit AUTO and let your legs do it.
  2. attack clonejacker. Twice, on two different clonejackers. This is your first real fight and it's meant to be winnable.
  3. loot the bodies until you find Grady's cells.
  4. Walk back and hand them over.

He pays ₵120, some jerky and bandages, and a lucky flashlight. Take note of that flashlight. It isn't a souvenir.

Down the Drain

Because his next problem is underground. The storm drain on Ironside is backing up with vermin, and vermin work their way up — toward the vats where people like you're grown.

So you go down the rusted ladder into the Under, the sewer level beneath the city, and cull six of the things living in it. Rats, roaches, slimes; they all count. It pays ₵180.

Notice what Grady just did The flashlight from the first job is the equipment for the second, and going down without a light will show you why. The city hands you a tool, then a reason.

After that

  1. stats — see what's draining. Spend some of Grady's money on food and water before it matters.
  2. Buy something to wear and something to hit with. equip both.
  3. Head for the franchise strip job board — rotating legal gigs, small money, no way to die. Counting rats, delivering parcels, sitting in a seat someone needs filled.
  4. Bank what you don't need to carry. deposit at any ATM.
  5. Walk down to the South Gate and look at it — the guards, the turrets, the killing ground past the Curtain. Don't walk through. That's for a later night, with a full pack and someone else along.

A day in the life

Much later, when the city is yours

None of this is hour one. It's what an ordinary day looks like a long way in, here so you know what's out there. A city day runs eight real hours, so what follows is an evening or two of play, not a career.

  1. 06:40your flat

    Wake up somewhere that's yours

    You come round in a rented unit with the door locked behind you, which is already a different game from sleeping rough. Sleep ran its recovery; if you dreamt, that was its own thing — three or four rooms generated just for you and dissolved when you woke, while your body lay here in the dark being lootable.

    x mirror · shower · stand
  2. 07:10the kitchen

    Coffee, and the news

    brew something hot — it stays hot on a derived timer, so don't leave it. On the tablet: overnight headlines and the seven-day forecast. There's a ⚠ on Thursday. You make a note to have the coat by then.

    brew coffee · tablet
  3. 08:30the strip

    Work, of whatever kind you've chosen

    Maybe it's honest: a shift, a job off the board, a shopfront you hold the deed to, with staff you hired and stock you ordered. Maybe you fly — freight, medevac, VIP charter, or the ones that don't file a flight plan. Maybe it's a corp, and your day is territory and influence and somebody else's treasury.

    work · wares · stock · takeoff · corp
  4. 11:00out past the rim

    Get something out of the ground

    Fishing off the quay with the right rod, mining a deposit with the right tool, scavenging a ruin on a perpetual search that runs until it empties out or something interrupts it. All three are postures: you start them, and they keep going while you watch the water.

    fish · mine · scavenge
  5. 13:20your kitchen again

    Cook properly, for once

    Not a ration bar. Something chopped and marinated and watched, off a recipe you worked out yourself and saved under a name only you use. The good stuff is worth real money to people who can't cook, and the very best of it can't be improvised — it has to be learned.

    chop · marinate · cook · stir · plate
  6. 15:00somewhere private

    The business you don't advertise

    A bench behind a cabinet that doesn't look like a lab. Synthesis, splicing, something curing. Or the other trades: a lock that isn't yours, a pocket that isn't yours, a network of eyes you've paid for reporting who walked where. All of it's witnessed by somebody eventually, and the city keeps a wanted level.

    synthesize · splice · pick · sneak · hub
  7. 18:00the bar

    A beer, and the game's on

    Sit down. Order. Drink it slowly, because that's the only way it works. The set in the corner has the Deadball game on and half the room is watching it — there's money on it, some of it yours. Somebody's at the piano and is actually playing it, key by key, on their own keyboard.

    sit · buy beer · watch tv · wager · play piano
  8. 20:30upstairs

    Cards, chess, or a worse idea

    A poker table with real seats and a host who deals. A neon chess set rendered in three-quarter view where the pieces stand up off the board. The casino, if you hate money. Or trading cards — packs from a machine, and the odd feeling of opening one to find a stranger who minted themselves at a terminal last week.

    sit · bet · move e4 · openpack
  9. 23:00the gate

    Out, if you're going out

    The Under for something specific. The badlands with people you trust. South through the Curtain into the Wildlands, which is the version of this where you write a will first. Full loot, live PvP, and things that hunt.

    south · attack · flee · loot
  10. 02:00or a cell

    However it ends

    Home to your own bed and a mess you'll have to mop yourself, because a room you own keeps its filth. Or Holding, with your gear in an evidence locker and a cell door somebody could hack. Or the vat, with everything you were carrying lying somewhere out in the dark for whoever finds it first.

    mop · lock · sleep
The point of all that There's no track you're supposed to be on. The pilot, the cook, the card sharp, the chemist and the person who just likes owning a nice flat are all playing the game correctly. Skills only go up by use, so you become whatever you keep doing.

When you're stuck

Five commands that always answer

helpThe full command list, always
mapWhere am I, and what's near
statsWhat's wrong with me
iWhat have I got
x <thing>What is that
If the screen isn't working for you Tablet → Settings → General → Display Mode, or type displaymode log. That folds the whole game down into one plain scrolling transcript: no panes, no panels, everything narrated in order. It's built for screen readers, and it's a real way to play: the minigames still run, called out in words. displaymode visual puts it back.

For everything else: try the verb. If it doesn't exist the game says so and you've lost nothing. Half of learning this city is discovering that fish, cook, mop and gossip all do something.
